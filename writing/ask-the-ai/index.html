Ask the AI How to Ask the AI

2026‑08‑05 · ai, habits

Aggressive Over-Asking

Trying something AI can't do costs you thirty seconds.

Skipping something it could have done costs you the entire task, silently, forever.

That asymmetry decides how much you get out of the machine. Most people are on the wrong side of it every day, and it never feels like anything.

The Claim

Everyone is trying to figure out what AI can do. That question answers itself over time.

The interesting variable is what you reach for when you hit a problem, and how fast.

Two people with the same subscription to the same model get wildly different things out of it. The gap is reflex.

One routes problems toward the machine by default. The other only occasionally remembers there's an alternative.

Adoption is habit formation wearing the costume of skill acquisition.

The Missed Task

You open a messy spreadsheet and start fixing it by hand.

You rename columns, normalize dates, remove duplicates. The work is boring, but it feels small enough to just do.

The model could have written the cleanup script and told you where the data was malformed. It never occurs to you to ask, and nothing tells you what you missed.

So the habit gets one rep stronger. Next time a task like this shows up, you'll do it by hand again.

Four Stages

Each step is a shift in what you believe the tool is for. The tool itself barely changes between steps.

01Substitution

You replace Google with a chatbot.

Same task, better interface: a smarter search engine that talks back. Most people stop here indefinitely.

02Delegation

Something breaks, and you discover the tool can write the email itself. It drafts the memo, builds the slide deck, debugs the function.

The shift usually arrives by accident: you asked for advice and got the finished artifact instead.

03Recursion

You want to offload something and don't know how to ask.

This used to be a hard stop: if you didn't know how to instruct the tool, you didn't use it.

Then you do the obvious-in-hindsight thing and ask the AI how to ask the AI.

This is the hinge of the whole ladder. Your own ignorance stops being a ceiling, because the tool starts teaching you to use it.

04Orchestration

You hand over work that spans steps, tools, and files.

Your job shifts from executing to defining what good looks like and checking whether you got it.

Attention replaces effort as the scarce resource.

The Actual Mechanism

Each of these transitions is a belief update. The capability was already there.

Someone at stage one sits and thinks, asks a colleague, or opens a search tab.

Someone at stage four has opened a chat before finishing the thought.

A power user is someone whose latency between "I have a problem" and "I could hand this to AI" has collapsed to near zero, while the range of problems that triggers the thought keeps expanding.

Both halves matter. Speed without range means you use it reflexively for the three things you have always used it for. Range without speed means you know it could help and remember an hour later.

Why People Get Stuck

Your sense of what AI is for freezes at your first impression.

Try it in 2023, get a confidently wrong answer, file it under "unreliable writing assistant," and your reflexes stay calibrated to a system that no longer exists.

Capability moves faster than habit, so the gap widens quietly.

You never notice, because failed assumptions are invisible.

Assume it can't do something, skip trying, and nothing happens. No error message, no feedback, no correction. The belief sits there getting more wrong.

What Follows From This

The correct policy is aggressive over-asking: deliberately attempt the thing you assume will fail.

For individuals: when you're about to do something manually, ask whether that's judgment or habit.

For organizations, prompt training is mostly the wrong intervention: it teaches stage-two behavior to people who never reach for the tool.

What transfers is watching someone at stage four work. Technique can be looked up.

A sense of what is askable has to be caught.
