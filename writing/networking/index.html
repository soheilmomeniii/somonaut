Meet, Keep, Ask: Networking in Three Stages

2026‑10‑17 · networking, trust

At sixteen, meeting new people felt like a hit of something. Every new person was a fresh dose of dopamine, so for a while I went out of my way to meet people in every field I could find. The proper word for it is networking.

It took me a long time to learn that networking is an attractive word for something that can be a hole you pour your time into. It isn't as useful as it looks from a distance.

But sometimes you have no choice. I came from a small town and didn't know anyone, anywhere. I had to try to connect with people. And once I'd done that for a while, the international events made me start the whole thing again from zero.

The rule I took from all of it, I call long-term game with long-term people. Building trust with people is harder than you think, and the more directly you go for it, the worse it works. It's a long road, maybe one to three years, and it has to be very indirect.

The result can be nothing at all. There's no guarantee you get anything back. Often you'd have gone further keeping your head down and working on your own thing. But sometimes it helps you in ways you won't believe.

I see networking in three stages:

Meeting is the easiest. Keeping the relationship and getting something out of it are much harder. It's a soft skill nobody really talks about, and when they do, it's mostly bullshit.

01Meet

I always explain networking with dating. About 80 percent of it is the same.

You either go for a cold approach, or you show up in the communities and places where you think the person you want will be, or you randomly start talking to someone and realise, oh, this is interesting.

It depends on the field. In crypto there are a few big events a year around the world. If you're in the right place at the right time, you can meet almost everyone you need and build the first layer.

The thing that matters most at events, and that most people skip: the message after. A connection made at an event stays alive for a day or two, then it's gone. The first 24 to 48 hours are the most important part of the whole thing, and nobody takes them seriously.

The message doesn't need to be special. Something that shows who you were and where you met, a reference to what you talked about. That's enough to turn you from "someone I met somewhere" into a name. Thirty connections from an event with no follow-up are zero a week later.

The second way is to find people like you inside those communities. The first thing I did to build my circle was make a list of every successful Iranian in crypto, and over two or three years I connected with almost all of them. At least the first stage.

Another example: a woman in her twenties in a specific field? Go find others like you. Age is a category that can work. Or people in your field who are also good at something else, say they love running. How you find that depends on the field. A shared point like that is extremely useful in stage two.

Cold approach is the message or the reply you send to someone's story. If they have no idea who you are, the win rate is very low. The more indirect, the better: ask a real question, or help them with something, then move the relationship forward slowly.

Is a cold DM always worth it? No, definitely not. But trying beats not trying. A lot of relationships start exactly that way: a short, respectful, direct message with enough context and a clear goal. Last year I messaged the founder of 6th Man, one of the good crypto funds, to ask if I could get into their private event in Abu Dhabi. I've sent ones like it to people like Mike Dudas.

How to message

I see this all the time: someone has a question and opens with "can I ask you a question?" instead of just asking it.

Everyone has so many messages and things going on that continuing a random conversation whose topic isn't even clear yet kills your chance of a reply.

Get to the point in the first message. Don't wrap the question. Ask it clearly, briefly, with enough context.

If they don't answer, you can remind them politely after a while. In English people often resend the message with "bumping this." People are busy, messages get lost, and a reminder brings it back.

But remember: people usually see your message. If they don't answer, there's probably a reason. A reminder is good. Chasing isn't.

There are other tricks too. Starting a podcast is one. The best-known Persian example is Soheil Alavi, whose Tabaghe 16 podcast connects him with almost every top Iranian in the world. At the very least it's a channel that can be extremely valuable. More on why in stage three.

never ask people for things. getting and keeping a relationship has to be built on giving.

Give so much value that when you do ask for something, it's easy to get.

And one simple but important thing: know the basics and do them. Speak well. Don't be weird. Be kind. Show yourself as a normal, good person, not a strange one.

02Keep

This is the hardest stage, and the longest.

You need to know what you have to offer. The more the better. Over time, by helping the other person, by being useful or skilled, by being valuable in general, you add to what you're worth in their head.

What if you have nothing? That's the question most people have at the start. No money, no network, no résumé. The answer is that valuable things aren't necessarily expensive:

  • Time. Doing something they need but can't be bothered to do.
  • Gathering and summarising information. If you follow a field seriously, just knowing what's going on is value.
  • Connecting people one step below you. You don't have to connect big names to be a connector.
  • Being reliable. Actually doing whatever you said you'd do is rare.

None of these need money or a network.

Sometimes the value is just asking how they are, over time. We're lonelier than you think. A bit of kindness, a small check-in, a simple conversation now and then can put you in someone's head more than you'd expect.

Being in someone's feed, so they think of you a little when they see your post or story, works well. You're showing yourself indirectly, and it can start a conversation. Following them back is a good sign too. It's honestly one of the reasons I keep a Telegram channel: a small piece of me stays in people's heads, and it might be useful later.

Keeping a relationship is hard. That's why a lot of people keep a list of who they're in touch with and message them every so often, for whatever reason, as a reminder: hey, I'm still here.

My suggestion is a three-month cycle. Not too much, not too little.

But you can't message 200 people every three months. It isn't practical and you'll get sick of it. The list needs layers.

A small inner circle, maybe ten people, you're really in touch with and don't need to plan for. A middle layer, maybe fifty, that the three-month cycle is for. And a sleeping layer, everyone else, once a year or whenever a real reason comes up.

There are also times you target one person to build trust with. That's different, and the cycle might be different. What I'm describing is for keeping a relationship over years: someone you think could matter later, or simply someone worth keeping.

Another important thing: know how to treat whom.

If someone just got very rich, or has the trauma of feeling everyone wants them for their money, and you want a relationship with them, don't let them pay, and don't talk about money at all. Over time, prove that you and this relationship aren't after their money.

With wealthy people in general, the more relaxed and generous you are with money, the better it usually goes. To them money is a number. Paying for something is a small favour to them, while the same favour the other way round can be worth far more.

Dealing with new money and old money is different in a lot of ways. It even depends on nationality.

And showing rich people that money isn't a worry for you, that you're not stressed about it and you're confident with it, matters a lot. That shows over time, not in one or two meetings.

03Ask

Getting a result from a relationship looks different in everyone's circle. But here are some general examples:

  • Using the other person's network: they connect you to someone they know.
  • Getting a job offer, or even becoming co-founders and starting something together.
  • Investing alongside them, or in their company.
  • Connecting two or more people from your own network in a way that helps both. That's a kind of value you give.

A strong network makes you valuable. Sometimes you need to know how to prove you have one, indirectly. I mean it: indirectly.

How do you actually do it? Honestly, it can be very random. But imagine you've been in touch with someone for two or three years, getting an update every so often. At some point they need someone with a specific skill, and you're exactly that person. Who do you think they message first?

Or if you want to start a company or raise money, and you've built trust and proven yourself, you can raise from that same circle.

Results take time. The company I just joined came from a relationship I built in November 2024, two years ago. Several of my good friends abroad came from seeing each other at two or three events, each time in a different part of the world. And from being around the same age.

These results really are random and lucky. But if you manage quantity and quality well, luck eventually knocks.

How you ask matters a lot. You need to know the person's patterns and character. And what you've planted about yourself in their head matters a lot.

You need to have done enough for them that if you ask for something small, they'll do it. Many requests are small for them and life-changing for you.

Knowing what isn't a big deal for them but is a big deal for you, and what you have that they don't and can give them, matters more than everything else here.

Sometimes you need skills and relationships from outside work to get close to someone, and only then get something work-related out of it.

Maybe the best result of all is becoming friends with people in your circle and learning from them. It doesn't pay directly, but it's extremely useful. It's my favourite one.

Collect or build

Back to where I started: networking can be a hole, and often it is.

The difference is whether you're collecting relationships or building them. Collecting means names, followers and business cards, which are worth nothing. Building means a few years, a few people, and actually being someone to them.

few but real beats many and hollow.
